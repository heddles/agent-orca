# Epidemiology Causal Patterns

## Common Study Designs for Causal Inference

### Cohort Studies
Follow groups with different exposures over time to compare outcomes.
Strength: Direct measurement of incidence rates
Weakness: Expensive, time-consuming, loss to follow-up

### Case-Control Studies
Compare exposure history between cases (with outcome) and controls.
Strength: Efficient for rare outcomes
Weakness: Cannot estimate incidence, prone to recall bias

### Nested Case-Control
Cases and controls drawn from a cohort, reducing selection bias.

## Key Causal Questions in Epidemiology

### 1. Confounding by Indication
When treatment assignment depends on patient characteristics that also affect outcome.
Example: Sicker patients get a new drug, making the drug appear harmful.

### 2. Healthy User Bias
Health-conscious people engage in multiple healthy behaviors.
Example: Exercise appears protective, but may reflect overall healthy lifestyle.

### 3. Collider Stratification
Conditioning on a collider (like hospitalization) can induce associations.
Example: Among hospitalized patients, smoking appears protective against COVID mortality.

## Causal Inference Methods in Epidemiology

### Propensity Score Methods
Match, stratify, or weight on propensity scores to balance confounders.

### Marginal Structural Models
Handle time-varying treatments and confounders with inverse probability weighting.

### Mendelian Randomization
Use genetic variants as instrumental variables for modifiable exposures.

### Mediation Analysis
Decompose total effect into direct and indirect effects through mediators.

## Effect Measures

- Risk Ratio (RR): P(Y=1|X=1) / P(Y=1|X=0)
- Odds Ratio (OR): Odds of outcome with exposure / without exposure
- Risk Difference (RD): Absolute risk difference
- Hazard Ratio (HR): Ratio of hazard rates in survival analysis
