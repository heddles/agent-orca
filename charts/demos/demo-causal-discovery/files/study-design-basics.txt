# Study Design Basics for Causal Inference

## Observational vs Experimental Studies

### Experimental (RCTs)
- Random assignment eliminates confounding
- Can establish causation with high confidence
- Expensive and sometimes unethical

### Observational
- Natural variation in exposure
- Must control for confounding statistically
- Most common in practice

## Key Design Principles

### 1. Temporal Precedence
Cause must come before effect.
- Prospective studies: follow subjects forward
- Retrospective studies: verify timing in records

### 2. Plausible Mechanism
Biological or theoretical rationale for causation.

### 3. Dose-Response Relationship
More exposure leads to more effect.

### 4. Consistency
Effect seen across different populations and methods.

## Threats to Causal Inference

### Selection Bias
Groups differ systematically at baseline.
- Solution: Random sampling, matching, propensity scores

### Information Bias
Measurement error in exposure or outcome.
- Solution: Validated measures, blinded assessment

### Confounding Bias
Third variable affects both exposure and outcome.
- Solution: Restriction, matching, adjustment, IV

## Minimum Data Requirements

1. **Exposure/Treatment**: Clearly defined and measured
2. **Outcome**: Clearly defined and measured
3. **Confounders**: All common causes measured
4. **Sample Size**: Adequate for detecting effect
5. **Follow-up**: Sufficient time to observe outcome

## Sensitivity Analysis Checklist

- How strong would unmeasured confounding need to be?
- Are results robust to different model specifications?
- Do results change when excluding outlier observations?
- Is there effect modification by key covariates?
